How this compares

Five alternatives, and what each of them is actually good at.

Comparison pages are usually written to win, which is why nobody believes them. Each of these opens by saying what the alternative is genuinely better at.

Most are not rivals Harnesses, search tools and model providers plug into this over MCP. A layer underneath is a different purchase from a tool on top.
The test is what you keep Remove each vendor in turn and ask what your company still has on Monday. That question sorts this market faster than a feature grid.
We will name names on a call These pages argue about categories. A specific vendor deserves a specific answer, and one that can be corrected if we have it wrong.

Where everything sits

Everyone owns one band, and the middle ones are the reason you are still stuck.

These are different categories doing different jobs, and each is good at its own.

Pick the one you are actually weighing

Five comparisons.

Build it yourself

Your platform team could build this

They could, and some of it they should. The question is which parts are differentiating for you and which are the same infrastructure every company in this position needs.

“We have good engineers. Why buy?”

Data platforms

The warehouse or lakehouse you already pay for

They are right that agent quality is a context problem. Their answer to it needs the data inside their platform first, and that precondition is the reason this is still unsolved for you.

“Why not use what we already have?”

Search and assistants

Enterprise search, memory layers, company assistants

Genuinely good at what your company wrote: documents, tickets, wikis, threads. A different problem from what your company runs, and from acting inside it.

“Doesn't this already do company brain?”

Agent platforms

Agent builders, automation platforms, AI employees

Fast to a working agent, and often the right buy for one bounded job. What is usually missing is the floor underneath, and an answer to who owns it in year two.

“They demo an agent in an afternoon.”

Wait for the models

Do nothing for eighteen months

A real option, and most of what you would buy today does get cheaper if you wait. What does not arrive on its own is the part about your own company.

“Won't the models just absorb this?”

What you get that the others do not give you

Three things you end up with, once the shared claims are set aside.

Profiling your systems, letting non-engineers build, bringing your own model and installing on-premise are all on several competitors' home pages today, so none of them tells you much. These three are what is left.

01

Your own team runs it

Every serious vendor here starts with their engineers sitting beside yours, and we do too. The difference is what it is designed to become: your own domain experts authoring the skills, writing the rules and running the layer, with our people gone. Ask any vendor what their delivery cost looks like on the second engagement compared to the first, and ask us the same.

02

You get a capability, not one outcome

Buy a finished agent and you own that workflow. Buy this and your team can build the next twenty without calling anyone, because the connectors, the permissions and the deploy path are already there. Slower to feel good in month one, and a different thing to own in year two.

03

It learns from your real work

Vendors building places to train and test agents nearly all construct a synthetic copy of common software. A copy cannot contain twelve years of exceptions in your order to cash, because nobody ever wrote them down. This runs against your real systems while your people do real work, so what accumulates is genuinely yours.
